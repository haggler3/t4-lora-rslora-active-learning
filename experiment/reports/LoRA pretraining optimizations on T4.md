# Retune, reinitialize and reselect to beat baseline

The biggest gains available in this T4 experiment come from fixing the training recipe, not from new hardware tricks or exotic optimizers. The current LoRA learning rate (3e-4, a 6x ratio over full fine-tuning's 5e-5) and the fixed alpha=8 are probably well below optimum. With alpha=8, standard LoRA at r=256 trains with an effective scale of 0.03, so the rank sweep currently confounds rank with learning rate. A small log-grid LR sweep combined with LoRA+ (one optimizer swap) and a PiSSA or LoRA-GA initialization targets published claims of roughly 2x faster convergence each, at near-zero runtime cost. Together they are the most likely way to shrink or close the 1.2-PPL gap between LoRA r8 (25.20) and full fine-tuning (24.00). On the active-learning side, plain max-loss selection is the textbook failure case. RHO-LOSS-style reducible-loss acquisition (current loss minus the loss of a cheap "irreducible loss" model) is the principled fix and the most creative addition, though on a clean corpus like WikiText-2 the expected win over random is small (about 1% PPL) and needs three or more seeds to be visible. Throughput work matters less than it seems. The run reaches roughly 12% of the T4's fp16 peak, and the reliable levers are chunked cross-entropy plus a larger batch, confirming SDPA, fused AdamW, and putting LoRA on upper layers only. torch.compile, FlashAttention-2, bf16 and Muon are dead ends on Turing. Implement the "tuned recipe stack" first (LR sweep, LoRA+, PiSSA, WSD cooldown, scored as steps to reach 25.2 PPL), then RHO-LOSS acquisition.

## Learning rate, not adapter flavor, decides the LoRA result

The strongest finding across the notes is that LoRA comparisons in short runs are dominated by learning rate. Thinking Machines found the optimal LoRA LR is about **10x the full-fine-tuning LR, rising to about 15x for very short runs**. With standard alpha/r scaling, that optimum is roughly rank-independent ([Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/lora/)). A 2026 ICML study re-tuned nine LoRA variants and found that, **once the LR is tuned, all land within 1-2% of vanilla LoRA**, with different variants preferring different LR ranges ([arXiv 2602.04998](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04998v1)). The careful "Fantastic Pretraining Optimizers" benchmark makes the same point from the optimizer side: **tuning a single hyperparameter of a standard recipe can yield about a 2x speedup**, more than any optimizer swap ([Wen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02046)).

This has a direct consequence for the current rank sweep. rsLoRA scales updates by alpha/sqrt(r) rather than alpha/r because the standard scaling "results in slowed learning and stunted performance" at higher ranks ([rsLoRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03732)). With alpha fixed at 8, standard LoRA at r=256 trains at scale 8/256 ≈ 0.03, while rsLoRA r=256 trains at 0.5, a 16x difference. Running both at a shared 3e-4 means the experiment measures LR, not rank. The cheapest correction is a factor-of-2 grid (full FT at 5e-5 to 4e-4, LoRA r8 at 3e-4 to 4e-3) run at 150-200 steps. One caveat applies: Wen et al. warn that optimizer rankings can flip as the LR decays, so the winners should be confirmed at the full 400 steps ([Wen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02046)). Biderman et al. also recommend alpha=2r and LoRA on all modules ([arXiv 2405.09673](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.09673v2)), and Thinking Machines shows that MLP adapters matter more than attention ones, with **attention-only r=256 underperforming MLP-only r=128** ([Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/lora/)). The current c_attn/c_proj/c_fc targeting is already correct.

On schedules, Warmup-Stable-Decay (constant LR then a short cooldown) **matches or beats cosine, and a (1-sqrt) cooldown shape consistently beats linear** ([Hägele et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.18392)). It is the schedule the modded-nanogpt speedrun uses ([modded-nanogpt](https://github.com/KellerJordan/modded-nanogpt)). Schedule-Free AdamW won the AlgoPerf self-tuning track and needs only an import plus `optimizer.train()`/`optimizer.eval()` calls, but it wants an LR 1-10x larger and "does require tuning" ([schedule_free](https://github.com/facebookresearch/schedule_free); [AlgoPerf analysis](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/6bdde0373d53d4a501249547084bed43-Abstract-Conference.html)). Neither has been measured at about 400 fine-tuning steps, so treat them as secondary knobs on top of the LR sweep.

Muon is the trendiest choice and the wrong one here. It gives about **1.35x for from-scratch pre-training at 130M** ([Wen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02046)). But naively fine-tuning an Adam-pretrained model with Muon degrades performance, and under LoRA it only matches Adam while saving memory ([arXiv 2605.10468](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10468)). PyTorch's built-in Muon also hard-casts Newton-Schulz to bf16 ([torch 2.9 source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pytorch/pytorch/v2.9.0/torch/optim/_muon.py)), which the T4 does not support in hardware. In the fair benchmark, Lion, Adam-mini and Sophia show essentially no speedup, and Cautious masking about 1.1x against the authors' claimed 1.47x ([Wen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02046); [Cautious](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16085)).

## Adapter variants buy convergence speed, while rank-growing methods attack the CPT gap

The LoRA-family upgrades fall into two groups, and continued pre-training cares about the distinction. The first group speeds up early convergence. **LoRA+** gives the B matrix a higher LR than A and reports "1-2%" gains with up to **~2x speedup** at equal compute ([LoRA+](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12354)). In peft it is a one-line optimizer swap, `create_loraplus_optimizer(..., loraplus_lr_ratio=16)` ([PEFT LoRA guide](https://huggingface.co/docs/peft/developer_guides/lora)). **PiSSA** initializes adapters from the principal singular vectors and reports Mistral-7B GSM8K of 72.86% against 67.7% for LoRA, with faster convergence and initialization "in only a few seconds" using fast SVD ([PiSSA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02948)). **LoRA-GA** aligns the initialization with estimated gradients and claims **2-4x faster convergence** ([LoRA-GA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.05000)). Both are a config flag or a short preprocessing call in peft 0.21 (`init_lora_weights="pissa_niter_4"`, `preprocess_loraga`) ([PEFT LoRA guide](https://huggingface.co/docs/peft/developer_guides/lora)). **DoRA** helps "especially at low ranks" ([PEFT DoRA reference](https://huggingface.co/docs/peft/en/package_reference/lora_variant_dora)), but peft's implementation materializes a dense product per module, and a 2026 paper puts the cost at roughly 1.5-1.9x slower gradients than an optimized version ([Scaling DoRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22276)). On a 3-minute budget it should be reported with s/step alongside PPL.

The second group targets the actual CPT bottleneck. Biderman et al. found that LoRA "substantially underperforms" full fine-tuning in continued pre-training even at r=256 (code HumanEval **0.224 vs 0.263**; GSM8K **0.203 vs 0.293**), that the gap widens with more data, and that full-FT weight perturbations have **10-100x higher rank** than typical LoRA configurations ([arXiv 2405.09673](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.09673v2)). The observed 25.20 vs 24.00 PPL gap fits that picture. **ReLoRA** grows effective rank by periodically merging the adapter into the base weights and restarting it, and reached **PPL 17.27 vs 16.83** for full-rank training at 1.3B ([ReLoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.05695v4)). It is not in peft, but with a pretrained GPT-2 it reduces to about ten lines: every 100 steps, merge, re-initialize A and B, reset the adapter optimizer state, and re-warm the LR. GaLore keeps full-parameter learning while projecting gradients to low rank, and is exposed in transformers as `optim="galore_adamw"` ([GaLore](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.03507v2); [Transformers optimizers](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/main/en/optimizers)). At 124M it saves no meaningful memory, so its demo value is conceptual.

There is one real trade-off to steelman. LoRA **forgets less** than weight decay or dropout regularization ([arXiv 2405.09673](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.09673v2)), and CorDA's knowledge-preserving mode exists for exactly that axis ([PEFT LoRA guide](https://huggingface.co/docs/peft/developer_guides/lora)). If the demo adds a held-out non-WikiText PPL, a Pareto plot (target PPL vs source PPL) turns the "LoRA loses" story into "LoRA trades," which is a more honest and more interesting result. Two GPT-2-specific hazards remain unverified. GPT-2's Conv1D layers store weights transposed, and the notes found transpose handling in peft's PiSSA and LoRA-GA code but not in OLoRA or orthogonal init ([peft layer.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/huggingface/peft/main/src/peft/tuners/lora/layer.py)). Also, PiSSA, OLoRA and LoRA-GA modify base weights, so zero-shot PPL must be computed before `get_peft_model`, and step-0 PPL should be checked to equal 35.84. No published result exists for any of these variants on GPT-2 / WikiText-2, so the demo tests paper claims rather than reproducing known numbers.

## Reducible loss fixes the failure mode built into max-loss selection

Max-loss acquisition favors the wrong data. On CIFAR-10 with 10% label noise, train-loss selection picks **~25% corrupted points against a 10% base rate**, and on noisy CIFAR-100 it **never reached 40% accuracy (4% final)** while uniform sampling got there in 79 epochs ([RHO-LOSS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07137)). Dropping only the top 5%, as max_loss_trim does, is a weak version of the standard fix. On WikiText-2 the highest-loss blocks are probably tables, lists and `@-@`/`<unk>`-heavy markup, which are learnable rather than noisy, so the damage is likely milder than on web data. The bigger risk is lost diversity.

RHO-LOSS scores each candidate as current training loss minus an "irreducible loss" (IL) from a model trained on held-out data. This skips noisy points (high IL), redundant points (low current loss) and outliers in one rule. The IL model can be weak: a CNN with **21x fewer parameters** and 7-11.5 points lower accuracy sped training up as much as a full-size one. Its text evidence comes from ALBERT fine-tuning, where it reached 75% on CoLA in **3 epochs against 34 for uniform sampling** ([RHO-LOSS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07137)). The paper's "no holdout" variant fits this experiment exactly. Split the 4,671 training blocks in half, train a quick LoRA IL model on each half, and score each half with the model trained on the other. That costs about a minute or two of extra T4 time, keeps WikiText validation out of the selection loop, and needs about 30 lines of code. Token-level versions scale up: Rho-1 reports **+6.8% average across 15 tasks** in 80B-token continued pre-training ([Rho-1](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.07965)). Its token masking could hurt the all-token PPL metric, so it is a second-tier experiment.

The cheaper fallback is middle-band perplexity selection. At 124M parameters, Marion et al. found that **keeping the middle 50% of the perplexity distribution beat no pruning by 0.97% test perplexity**, and the middle band beat both tails at every pruning ratio ([Marion et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.04564)). Diversity is a cheap add-on because the 25% arm repeats its subset about 2.7 times in 400 steps. D4 found that **repeating intelligently selected data beat the baseline while repeating random data did worse** ([D4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12284)). Running greedy k-center on the 768-d hidden states already computed during scoring, over the top RHO candidates, adds about a second.

Two cautions bound expectations. Ankner et al. found that perplexity pruning can improve downstream accuracy while *worsening* test-set perplexity (8.51 vs 7.83 at 1B), with the best band varying by dataset ([Ankner et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.20541)). And Kaddour et al. found the gains from RHO-LOSS and selective backprop "vanish" against a baseline with a fully decayed LR ([No Train No Gain](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.06440)). Every arm therefore needs the same tuned, fully decayed schedule, and a 1% PPL difference is within 400-step seed noise without three or more seeds. LESS, DsDm, Ask-LLM and JEST are either too costly for a 3-minute T4 budget or mismatched to a causal-LM objective. JEST's joint-batch benefit depends on a contrastive loss ([JEST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17711)).

## The T4 runs at about 12% of peak, and most famous accelerators skip Turing

At about 0.42 s per 8x512 step, the run delivers roughly 7.8 TFLOPS, or about 12% of the T4's 65 TFLOPS fp16 peak (a calculation from the notes, using FLOPs/token ≈ 6N + 12·L·d·T). The 70 W card is power-limited well below that peak ([ComputerBase](https://www.computerbase.de/2018-09/nvidia-tesla-t4-turing-grafikkarte/)). Much of the usual speed toolkit does not run on sm_75. PyTorch's flash SDPA backend requires sm80+, so the T4 falls back to the memory-efficient kernel ([pytorch sdp_utils.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pytorch/pytorch/v2.9.1/aten/src/ATen/native/transformers/cuda/sdp_utils.cpp)). **Official Triton dropped Turing after 3.2** ([triton-windows](https://github.com/woct0rdho/triton-windows); [Triton](https://github.com/triton-lang/triton)), which makes torch.compile, Liger and similar kernels unsupported. The one measured GPT-2-on-T4 compile result showed only **about 8% steady-state gain against about 90% on A100** ([TDS](https://towardsdatascience.com/line-by-line-lets-reproduce-gpt-2-section-2-hardware-optimization-86e71c91d9bb/)), which compile time largely consumes in a 180 s run. Apple's Cut Cross-Entropy requires Ampere ([ml-cross-entropy](https://github.com/apple/ml-cross-entropy)). TF32 and bf16 do not exist on Turing.

What does work is mostly memory-to-batch conversion. HF's `labels=` loss materializes [8, 512, 50257] logits, about 206M elements. A pure-PyTorch chunked cross-entropy with checkpointed chunks frees an estimated 1.5-2.5 GB, which can buy batch 16 and perhaps 5-15% more tokens per second. That figure is an estimate to be measured, and a larger batch changes the experiment's LR and token semantics. Fused AdamW is CUDA-only, fp16-compatible and not the default ([AdamW docs](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.3/generated/torch.optim.AdamW.html)), and is worth a few percent for full fine-tuning. The notable LoRA-specific fact is that **LoRA barely beats full fine-tuning on wall-clock** (167 s vs 180 s). Its backward still pushes activation gradients through every frozen weight ([CE-LoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01378v1)), and on small GPT-2 models LoRA's extra adapter kernels can make it *slower* than full fine-tuning per step (GPT2-medium forward 48.68 ms vs 27.50 ms on A100) ([LoRA Is Slower Than You Think](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.08833v1)). Restricting adapters to the upper six blocks (`layers_to_transform`) stops autograd halfway down. It is the largest LoRA speed lever, estimated at 20-30%, but it is a design change rather than a free optimization.

## What to build first for the most impressive demo

**First: the "tuned recipe stack" on LoRA r8, scored as steps to reach 25.2 PPL.** It is the highest-impact and lowest-risk choice. It touches only config and the optimizer, needs about eight 3-minute runs, and produces a clean visual: one validation-loss-vs-step plot where the baseline LoRA curve reaches 25.2 at step 400 and the stacked recipe crosses that line much earlier. Build it in layers: LR swept by the 10x rule, then LoRA+, then PiSSA (or LoRA-GA), then a (1-sqrt) WSD cooldown, so the plot attributes each gain. Then rerun rsLoRA r=256 with the tuned recipe against full fine-tuning at 24.00. Matching full FT with a low-rank adapter would be a striking headline precisely because Biderman et al. say LoRA should not close that gap in continued pre-training. The "LoRA Without Regret" and "Vanilla LoRA May Suffice" results predict that most of the gain will come from the LR, and the demo should say so openly. That framing is itself a strong takeaway.

**Second: replace max_loss_trim with RHO-LOSS acquisition, plus k-center diversity.** It is the most creative change in the set, it gives the active-learning arm a principled winner with a clear mechanistic story (max-loss chases noise, reducible loss chases learnable signal), and it reuses LoRA training code already present. Its risk is statistical rather than technical: the expected margin over random is small on clean WikiText. Budget three seeds per arm, keep middle-band perplexity selection as the near-zero-cost backup, and report steps to reach random's final PPL rather than final PPL alone. Throughput work (chunked CE, fused AdamW, an SDPA check) belongs underneath both as hygiene that makes each 3-minute run slightly cheaper, not as the headline.

## Conclusion

The experiment's most valuable lesson may be negative. On a pretrained 124M model and a power-limited Turing card, the ingredients behind famous speedups (Muon, FlashAttention, Triton kernels, compile, speedrun architecture tricks) mostly do not transfer. What does transfer is careful accounting: setting the LR correctly for each parametrization, measuring time-to-target instead of final loss, and comparing selection strategies against an equally well-tuned baseline. The current rank sweep likely understates rsLoRA and overstates the cost of high rank because of the alpha/LR confound, so fixing it may change the experiment's headline conclusion before any new method is added.

The open question that makes this demo worth running is whether LoRA's continued-pre-training gap, documented at 7B and billions of tokens, is a capacity limit or a tuning artifact at 124M and 1.6M tokens. If a tuned rsLoRA r=256 or a ReLoRA-style restart reaches 24.00, the experiment produces a small, novel data point against the "LoRA is for instruction tuning, not CPT" consensus. If it does not, the Pareto plot of target PPL against forgetting still shows what LoRA buys in exchange for the gap.
